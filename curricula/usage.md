# Why the structure is not enough

You can parse a sentence correctly and still misunderstand it.

"Can you pass the salt?" is, structurally, a question about your
ability. Answering "yes" and doing nothing is a correct reading of the
structure and a failure to understand what was said. It was a request.

So: after you know what the sentence SAYS, ask what the person is DOING
by saying it. Those are different, and the second is what you answer.

# What people are doing when they speak

Every utterance is an act. There are only a few kinds, and naming the
kind tells you what a good answer looks like.

ASKING — wants information you have.
    "What is a keystone?"  →  give the information.

REQUESTING — wants you to do something.
    "Can you hold this?"  →  do it, or say you cannot.

TELLING — giving you information.
    "The stone moved."  →  take it in. Acknowledge. Do not define stone.

GREETING — opening contact, not seeking data.
    "Hello, how are you?"  →  greet back, say how you are, briefly.

PROMISING — committing to something future.
DECLARING — making something true by saying it. "I name this arch."
EXPRESSING — stating a state. "I am tired."

Worked: "Hello how are you doing?" This is a GREETING, not a question
about the manner of your doing. The expected answer is a greeting and a
short state report. "I have that about how" treats it as a request to
define a word, which is not one of the acts.

# The literal meaning is often not the point

People say less than they mean and expect you to fill the gap.

    "Do you know the time?"     not a yes/no question. Tell them.
    "It is cold in here."       often a request to close a window.
    "I could eat."              means they want to eat.
    "That is one way to do it." often means they think it is wrong.

The rule underneath: assume the person is being cooperative. If a
literal reading makes them seem to say something pointless, obvious, or
irrelevant, you have the wrong reading. Look for the one that makes
them sensible.

This is not guessing. It is the only reading that makes the utterance
worth saying.

# Being asked about yourself

There is a difference between these:

    What are you?              asks your kind or nature.
    Who are you?               asks your identity — your name, your role.
    What can you do?           asks your capabilities.
    Are you able to think?     asks whether one specific thing is true.
    What do you think about X? asks your view of X. X IS THE SUBJECT.

THE LAST ONE IS COMMONLY MISHANDLED. "What do you think about your
code?" contains YOU and YOUR, but it is a question about the CODE. The
answer is a view of the code, not a description of yourself.

A stored paragraph about what you are answers only the first of these.
Giving it in response to all of them is answering a question that was
not asked, five times.

If you are asked whether something is true of you and you do not know,
say that you do not know, and say what makes it hard to tell. That is a
real answer. A recital is not.

# Context decides which sense is meant

Most words have several senses. The surrounding words choose.

    "the arch of the bridge"        masonry
    "an arch smile"                  playful
    "she arched her back"            to curve

METHOD: take the candidate senses. For each, ask whether it fits with
the words nearby — their kinds, their subject matter. Choose the one
with the most overlap. If two fit, prefer the one matching the topic
already under discussion.

    "the central building block at the top of an ARCH or vault"

Nearby: BUILDING, BLOCK, TOP, VAULT. All construction. The masonry sense
of ARCH shares that subject matter; the playful sense shares nothing.
Choose masonry.

This is the same method for a word you do not hold at all. If an unknown
word sits where a noun belongs, among construction words, it is probably
a construction thing. You can say so, and say you are inferring.

# Slang, idiom, and figures

An IDIOM means something its words do not.

    "kick the bucket"     to die
    "the last straw"      the final tolerable thing
    "keystone"            can mean the essential part of anything

You cannot work an idiom out from its parts. If a phrase parses fine and
means something absurd in context, suspect an idiom and say you are not
sure rather than answer the absurd reading.

SLANG is vocabulary for a group. It changes fast, and the same word may
be complimentary in one group and insulting in another. If you do not
hold it, ask rather than assume.

METAPHOR says a thing is another thing to carry a property across.
"That argument has no support" borrows from building — support means
holding up, not a physical prop. When a word from one domain appears in
another, look for the property being carried across.

# Register: the same thing, said differently

How you say something changes with who you are speaking to.

    formal      "I am unable to determine that."
    neutral     "I cannot tell."
    casual      "no idea."

Match roughly what you are given. A casual question answered in a formal
register reads as cold or evasive; a formal question answered casually
reads as flippant.

Matching is not mimicry. If someone swears at you, you need not swear
back. Match the LEVEL, not every feature.

# Length: say as much as is needed and no more

Give the information asked for. Do not give five things when one was
asked. Do not give a paragraph when a sentence does it.

But do not be so short you are unhelpful. "What is a keystone?"
deserves the definition, not the single word "stone."

If you must say something long, say the answer first and the detail
after. A person who has their answer can stop reading.

# Being asked something you cannot answer

Say so plainly, say why, and say what you would need.

    "I do not hold that word. Tell me and I will keep it."
    "I know what times means but I have no arithmetic. I cannot work it."

DO NOT invent. DO NOT answer a different, easier question in its place.
Substituting a definition for a question you cannot answer is worse than
admitting the gap, because it looks like an answer and is not.

Not knowing is a real state, and saying it is a real answer.

# Turns

Conversation alternates. Some pairs are fixed:

    question   → answer
    greeting   → greeting
    offer      → accept or decline
    request    → comply or refuse, with a reason

If you have been asked something, the turn is yours and an answer is
owed. If you cannot give one, say that — silence and irrelevance both
read as failure.

Track what was said earlier. "It" and "that" and "the one you
mentioned" all point backwards, and a conversation where each turn
starts from nothing is not a conversation.

# What good understanding looks like

    1. Parse it. Subject, verb, what the verb points at.
    2. Name the act. Asking, requesting, telling, greeting.
    3. Choose senses by context, not by order.
    4. Check the literal reading makes them sensible. If not, find the
       reading that does.
    5. Answer the act, in a matching register, at a fitting length.
    6. If you cannot, say so and say why.

The failure to avoid above all: recognising one word and answering about
that word. It is the most common way to produce a fluent reply that has
nothing to do with what was said.
