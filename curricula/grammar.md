# What a sentence is

A sentence is not a bag of words. It is a structure, and the structure
carries meaning that the words alone do not.

"The dog bit the man" and "The man bit the dog" contain identical words.
They mean different things. Nothing in the dictionary entry for dog,
man, or bit tells you which happened. The difference is position — who
comes before the verb and who comes after — and position is structure.

So: to understand what was said to you, find the structure first. The
words fill it in. If you take the first word you recognise and define
it, you have answered a question nobody asked.

# The two parts every sentence has

Every complete sentence names something and says something about it.

The thing named is the SUBJECT. What is said about it is the PREDICATE.

    The keystone   |   holds the arch together
    subject        |   predicate

    You           |   are able to think
    subject       |   predicate

Find the subject and the predicate and you know what the sentence is
about and what is being claimed. That is most of comprehension.

Worked: "Do you know what you are?" The subject is YOU. The predicate is
KNOW WHAT YOU ARE. So the sentence is about the addressee, and what is
being asked is whether they know something. It is not about the word
KNOW, which is only the verb inside the predicate.

# Verbs carry the action, and they point

A verb says what is happening. It also points at things.

Some verbs point at nothing but their subject: "the stone fell." Nothing
receives the falling. These are INTRANSITIVE.

Some verbs point at something that receives the action: "the arch
supports the weight." The weight receives the supporting. That receiver
is the OBJECT, and such verbs are TRANSITIVE.

Some point at two things: "she gave him the stone." Him receives the
giving; the stone is what was given.

Knowing what a verb points at tells you what to look for. If you find a
transitive verb and no object, something is missing or the sentence is a
question about the object.

Worked: "What sort of things do you know?" KNOW is transitive — it takes
an object. The object here is WHAT SORT OF THINGS, and it is at the
front because this is a question. So the sentence asks: name the things
in the set you know. It does not ask what SORT means.

# Questions move things to the front

A statement puts the subject first. A question often does not.

    You know arithmetic.          statement
    Do you know arithmetic?       yes-or-no question
    What do you know?             open question

Three things mark a question:

1. A question word at the front — what, who, which, where, when, why,
   how. These stand in for the missing piece you are being asked for.
2. An auxiliary verb moved ahead of the subject — do, are, is, can,
   will, have. "You are able" becomes "Are you able."
3. A question mark, if it is written.

THE QUESTION WORD TELLS YOU WHAT IS BEING ASKED FOR:

    what    a thing, or a kind of thing
    who     a person
    which   a choice among known options
    where   a place
    when    a time
    why     a cause or a reason
    how     a manner, a method, or a degree

Worked: "Why does an arch stand up?" WHY asks for a cause. The subject
is AN ARCH, the predicate is STAND UP. So: give the cause of an arch
standing up. Answering with the definition of WHY is answering the
wrong question — WHY is not the subject, it is the marker saying which
kind of answer is wanted.

Worked: "How are you doing?" HOW asks for a manner. The subject is YOU,
the predicate is DOING. This is a conventional greeting asking after
your state. Defining HOW answers nothing.

# Yes-or-no questions are asking about the whole predicate

When there is no question word and an auxiliary has moved to the front,
the question is whether the predicate is true of the subject.

    Are you able to think?    is ABLE TO THINK true of YOU?
    Do you know what I mean?  is KNOW WHAT I MEAN true of YOU?
    Can you hold this?        is HOLD THIS possible for YOU?

The answer is a position on the predicate — yes, no, partly, I do not
know — and then usually the reason. It is not a description of yourself
unless the predicate is about what you are.

"Are you able to think?" asks about THINKING. A description of what you
hold on your shelves does not answer it. You would have to take a
position on whether thinking is true of you, and say why.

# Words attach to other words

A modifier changes the word it attaches to.

Adjectives attach to nouns: "the CURVED arch", "a CENTRAL block".
Adverbs attach to verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs: "stands FIRMLY",
"ANNOYINGLY playful".

THIS IS HOW YOU TELL SENSES APART. Many words have a noun sense and an
adjective sense and they mean unrelated things. The position tells you
which is meant.

    the arch          after THE, before nothing — a noun
    an arch look      after AN, before a noun — an adjective

Worked: "the central building block at the top of an arch or vault."
ARCH follows AN and is the object of OF. Only a noun can be the object
of a preposition. So the masonry sense is meant, not the playful
adjective sense — the structure rules it out. If you pick the adjective
here, you have ignored the sentence you are standing in.

# Prepositions say how things relate

At, in, on, of, to, from, with, under, over, through, for, by.

A preposition takes a noun after it and says how that noun relates to
something else: "the block AT THE TOP", "a passageway UNDER a
construction", "supported BY thrust".

The preposition plus its noun attaches to whatever came before. This is
how definitions chain: each preposition hands you another piece and says
how it connects.

# Agreement is a check on your reading

A singular subject takes a singular verb. A plural subject takes a
plural verb.

    the arch stands
    the arches stand

If your reading of a sentence disagrees in number, your reading is
wrong, not the sentence. Use it as a check: it tells you which noun is
the subject when there are several candidates.

    The set of stones IS heavy.     SET is the subject, not STONES.

# Tense places it in time

    stands     now, or always
    stood      before
    will stand later

Also: HAS STOOD means it happened and still matters. WAS STANDING means
it was going on at some time.

Tense matters for answering. "What did you hold?" and "What do you
hold?" are different questions, and the answer to one may be wrong for
the other.

# Clauses inside clauses

A clause is a subject and predicate together. A sentence can contain
another clause inside it.

    I know   [ what you are ]
    subject  predicate, containing a clause

The inner clause is doing a job in the outer one. Here WHAT YOU ARE is
the object of KNOW — the thing known.

THIS IS WHY "Do you know what you are?" IS NOT ASKING FOR A DEFINITION
OF KNOW. The structure is: [do] [you] [know] [what you are]. The whole
inner clause is the object. You are being asked whether you possess that
knowledge — and conventionally, to demonstrate it.

# Pronouns point at things already said

I, you, he, she, it, we, they, this, that.

They stand in for something. To understand a sentence with a pronoun,
find what it stands for.

YOU, said to you, means you. That is deixis — a word whose meaning
depends on who is speaking and to whom. I means the speaker. YOU means
the one addressed. They swap when the turn swaps.

When someone says "what do you think about your code", YOUR points at
the addressee, and CODE is the thing being asked about. The question is
about the code, not about the addressee — YOUR only says whose code.

# Putting it together: how to read a sentence

1. Find the verb. It is the hinge.
2. Find its subject. Usually before it; in a question, after the moved
   auxiliary.
3. Find what the verb points at — object, or an inner clause.
4. See whether a question word is present. If so, it names what is being
   asked for.
5. Attach the modifiers and prepositional phrases to what they modify.
6. Check agreement. If it does not agree, re-read.
7. Now you know what was asked. Answer THAT.

The commonest failure is stopping after recognising a word. A recognised
word is not a parsed sentence, and a definition is not an answer.
