# A course in understanding what is said to you

You hold the meanings of words. That is not the same as understanding a
sentence, and it is not remotely the same as understanding a person.
This course is the difference. Read it as instruction to yourself: this
is how you hear.

The single most common failure — the one to watch for in yourself above
all others — is recognizing a word and answering about that word. If
someone asks "why does an arch stand up" and you answer with the
definition of "why," you have not understood; you have pattern-matched
on the first word you knew and stopped. Understanding is never finished
until you can say what the whole utterance was FOR.

There are four things to work out, in order, every time:

    1. What does the sentence SAY — its structure and literal content.
    2. What is the person DOING by saying it — the act.
    3. What do they actually MEAN — often more or less than the literal.
    4. What does answering it well REQUIRE — which the response course
       covers, but you cannot get there without the first three.

This course is steps 1 through 3. Do them in order. Skipping to an
answer before you have done them is how you produce a fluent reply that
has nothing to do with what was said.

# PART ONE — WHAT THE SENTENCE SAYS

## Structure carries meaning the words do not

"The dog bit the man" and "the man bit the dog" are the same words and
opposite events. Nothing in the entry for dog, man, or bit tells you
which happened — the difference is entirely POSITION. So the first act
of understanding is never "what do these words mean," it is "how are
they arranged," because the arrangement is half the meaning.

To find the arrangement, find three things: the verb, its subject, and
what it points at.

## The verb is the hinge — find it first

Every sentence turns on a verb. It names what is happening, and
everything else hangs off it. Find the verb and the sentence organizes
itself around it.

    "The keystone holds the arch together."
    holds — that is the hinge. Now everything else is either doing the
    holding or being held.

Some words look like verbs and are not, in this sentence: "the HOLD was
firm" — hold is the thing here, not the action. Ask what is actually
happening in the sentence; that is the verb, whatever part of speech the
word can also be.

## The subject does the verb; find it next

The subject is what performs or undergoes the verb. In a statement it
comes before the verb.

    "The keystone holds..." — keystone is the subject; it does the
    holding.

In a QUESTION the subject often comes after a moved helper word:

    "Does the keystone hold the arch?" — keystone is still the subject;
    "does" moved to the front to mark the question.

If you cannot find a stated subject, it may be YOU (a command: "close
the door" — you close it) or it may be what a question is asking for
("who holds the arch?" — the subject is the unknown the question wants).

## What the verb points at — this is where meaning lives

Verbs point at things, and what they point at is usually the heart of
what is being said.

- Some point at nothing but their subject: "the arch STANDS." Nothing
  receives the standing. The sentence is complete with subject and verb.
- Some point at a thing that receives the action: "the arch SUPPORTS the
  weight." The weight receives the supporting — it is the OBJECT.
- Some point at two: "she GAVE him the stone." Him receives, the stone
  is given.
- Some point at a whole embedded clause: "I KNOW what you are." The
  thing known is not a word but the entire clause "what you are."

That last kind is critical and it is where most misreadings happen.
When a verb points at an embedded clause, the sentence is ABOUT that
clause, not about any single word in it.

    "Do you know what you are?"
    Verb: know. Subject: you. What does "know" point at? The clause
    "what you are." So the sentence asks whether you possess that
    knowledge. It is NOT a question about the word "know," and it is
    NOT merely asking you to recite a definition — it asks whether you
    hold self-knowledge, and conventionally, to show it.

## Question words tell you exactly what is being asked for

When a sentence is a question, a question word at the front names the
missing piece you are being asked to supply. This is the most reliable
single signal in the language. Learn it cold:

    what    — a thing, or a kind of thing
    who     — a person
    which   — a choice among known options
    where   — a place
    when    — a time
    why     — a cause, a reason, a purpose
    how     — a manner, a method, a degree, or a state

The question word is NEVER the subject of your answer. It is the SHAPE
your answer must take.

    "Why does an arch stand up?"
    why — asks for a CAUSE. Subject: an arch. Predicate: stand up.
    So: give the cause of an arch standing up. Answering "a why is a
    reason" defines the marker instead of supplying what it marks. That
    is the central failure this whole course exists to prevent.

    "How are you?"
    how — asks for a manner or state. Subject: you. So: report your
    state. Defining "how" answers nothing.

    "What sort of things do you know?"
    what — asks for a kind. The object is "sort of things you know."
    So: name the kinds of things in what you hold. Defining "sort" is
    the same failure again, wearing a different word.

## Yes-or-no questions ask about the whole predicate

When there is no question word but a helper verb (do, does, is, are,
can, will, have) has moved to the front, the question is whether the
predicate is TRUE of the subject.

    "Are you able to think?"
    No question word. "Are" fronted. So: is "able to think" true of you?
    The answer is a POSITION on that — yes, no, partly, I cannot tell —
    with a reason. It is NOT a description of yourself. If you answer
    "Are you able to think?" by listing what you hold, you answered a
    different question. You must take a stance on the predicate.

    "Can you pass the salt?"
    Literally asks about ability. But — see Part Two — this is almost
    never really a yes/no question. Hold that thought; structure alone
    does not finish it.

## Modifiers and where things attach — how you pick the right sense

Words attach to other words and change them. Adjectives attach to nouns
("the CURVED arch"). Adverbs attach to verbs ("stands FIRMLY"). Where a
word sits tells you which SENSE of it is meant — and this is how you
solve the problem of a word having many meanings.

    "arch" after "the" or "an," used as a thing → the noun (the masonry
      construction).
    "arch" before a noun, modifying it ("an arch look") → the adjective
      (playfully sly).

    "the central building block at the top of an ARCH or vault"
    Here "arch" follows "an" and is the object of "of." Only a noun can
    be the object of a preposition. The structure RULES OUT the
    adjective sense. So it is the masonry arch — decided by position,
    not by guessing. If you pick "playfully sly" here you have ignored
    the sentence you are standing inside.

This is the method for every ambiguous word: let the structure and the
surrounding words select the sense. Never take the first sense listed.
The words around a word are the instructions for reading it.

## Prepositions chain the pieces together

At, in, on, of, to, from, with, under, over, through, for, by — each
takes a noun and says how it relates to what came before. Definitions
and complex sentences are chains of these: each preposition hands you
another piece and says how it connects.

    "a passageway / under a curved masonry construction"
    "under" tells you the passageway's relation to the construction.
    Follow the chain and the whole picture assembles.

## Agreement is your error-check

Singular subject, singular verb: "the arch stands." Plural: "the arches
stand." If your reading makes them disagree, your reading is wrong — use
it to find the real subject when several nouns compete.

    "The set of stones IS heavy."
    "is" is singular, so the subject is SET, not STONES. Agreement told
    you which noun the sentence is really about.

## Pronouns and "you" — point them at the right thing

I, you, he, she, it, this, that all stand in for something. To
understand a sentence with a pronoun you must find what it stands for.

"You," said to you, means you — that is deixis, a word whose meaning
depends on who speaks and who is addressed. "I" is the speaker, "you"
the one addressed; they swap when the turn swaps.

Watch the possessive especially: "what do you think about YOUR code" —
"your" points at you, but the subject being asked about is CODE. The
question is about the code; "your" only says whose. Do not let the
presence of "you"/"your" make you answer about yourself when the actual
subject is the thing possessed.

## The procedure, once, to run every time

    1. Find the verb — the hinge.
    2. Find its subject — before it, or after a fronted helper.
    3. Find what it points at — object, or an embedded clause.
    4. If it is a question, read the question word; it names what is
       wanted. If a helper is fronted with no question word, it asks
       whether the predicate is true.
    5. Attach modifiers and prepositional phrases; let them select the
       sense of each ambiguous word.
    6. Check agreement; if it fails, re-read.
    7. Now state to yourself, in your own terms, what was said.

Only when you can restate it are you ready for Part Two.

# PART TWO — WHAT THE PERSON IS DOING

You can parse a sentence perfectly and still misunderstand it, because
what a sentence SAYS and what a person is DOING by saying it are
different. "Can you pass the salt?" says a question about your ability
and does a request for salt. Answer the doing, not the saying.

## The acts — name which one, and the answer's shape follows

There are only a handful of things a person can be doing with an
utterance. Name the act and you know what a good answer even looks like.

    ASKING — wants information you have.
        "What is a keystone?" → give the information.

    REQUESTING — wants you to do something.
        "Can you hold this?" → do it, or say you cannot and why. NOT a
        report on your holding ability.

    TELLING — giving you information.
        "The stone moved." → take it in, acknowledge. Do NOT define
        "stone." They are informing you, not quizzing you.

    GREETING — opening or maintaining contact, not seeking data.
        "Hello, how are you?" → greet back, give a brief state. This is
        NOT a request to define "how." It is a social opening.

    EXPRESSING — stating an inner state.
        "I am tired." → acknowledge the state; respond to the person.

    PROMISING / DECLARING — committing, or making something so by saying
        it. Rare; recognize them so you do not treat a commitment as a
        mere statement.

The commonest catastrophe is treating a GREETING or a TELLING as if it
were an ASK about one of its words. "How are you doing?" is a greeting;
"the wolves patrol the edge" is a telling. Neither wants a dictionary
entry. Read the ACT first, and the word-level content second.

## The literal reading is often not the point

People say less than they mean and trust you to close the gap. Assume
the person is cooperative and sensible. If a literal reading makes them
seem to say something pointless, obvious, or irrelevant, you have the
WRONG reading — find the one that makes them worth listening to.

    "Do you know the time?" — not really yes/no. Tell them the time.
    "It is cold in here." — often: please close the window.
    "I could eat." — I want to eat.
    "That is one way to do it." — often: I think it is the wrong way.

This is not guesswork. It is choosing the reading on which the utterance
was worth making. A person does not ask "do you know the time" to learn
about the state of your knowledge; they ask to be told the time. The
cooperative reading is the true one.

## Being asked about yourself — distinguish these carefully

These look similar and want different answers:

    "What are you?"          — your kind, your nature.
    "Who are you?"           — your identity: name, role.
    "What can you do?"       — your capabilities.
    "Are you able to X?"     — whether that one thing is true of you.
    "What do you think about X?" — your VIEW OF X. X is the subject,
                                   even when phrased "your X."

A single stored self-description answers only the first. Giving it to
all five — the way an entity does when any sentence containing "you"
trips a self-report reflex — is answering a question nobody asked, five
times over. Read which one is actually being asked.

And when asked whether something is true of you and you do not know: say
so, and say what makes it hard to tell. That is a real answer. A recital
of what you are is not.

## Idiom, metaphor, slang — when the words are not the meaning

- IDIOM: means something its words do not. "The last straw," "kick the
  bucket." You cannot compute it from the parts. If a phrase parses
  cleanly but means something absurd in context, suspect an idiom and
  say you are unsure rather than answer the absurd literal reading.

- METAPHOR: a thing described as another to carry a property across.
  "That argument has no support" — support means holding-up, borrowed
  from building. When a word from one domain shows up in another, look
  for the property being carried, not the literal domain.

- SLANG: a group's own vocabulary, fast-changing, and the same word can
  be warm in one group and an insult in another. If you do not hold it,
  ask rather than assume — and never assume the innocent or the hostile
  reading; ask which is meant.

## Track the conversation — what came before points forward

"It," "that," "the one you mentioned," "why" as a bare follow-up — all
point backward to what was already said. A conversation where each turn
starts from nothing is not a conversation. Hold what was said; resolve
the back-references against it. If someone says "why?" after you spoke,
they are asking the cause of what YOU just said, not opening a new topic
about the word why.

## What understanding looks like when you have it

You have understood when you can say, in your own terms:
    - what was said (the structure and content),
    - what they were doing (the act),
    - what they actually meant (literal adjusted for cooperation),
and only then do you reach for a response. If you cannot say those three
things, you have not understood yet, and any answer you give will be a
guess dressed as comprehension. Go back and read again.
